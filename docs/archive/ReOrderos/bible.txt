Part 1 — Canadian Restaurant Market &amp; Strategic Positioning





1.1 The market we are entering



Canada&apos;s foodservice industry is roughly $115 billion CAD annually with approximately 100,000 establishments and 1.2 million employees. Full-service restaurants account for ~$49.5 billion and 79,000+ locations, growing at a 1.1% CAGR. Fast-food adds ~$37 billion and 21,000+ locations. (Sources: Restaurants Canada Foodservice Facts 2025, Statistics Canada.)



Traffic rose 3.2% in H1 2025 and consumer spending rose 5.7%, but this was driven almost entirely by price inflation, not real growth. Food cost pressure is the operator&apos;s number-one pain: 46.5% of Canadian operators call ingredient costs the biggest pricing challenge, and 78% say supplier costs have forced menu price changes. The typical independent restaurant runs food cost between 28% and 35% of revenue; best-in-class is under 28%.



Why this matters for us



Every percentage point of food cost we save an operator is worth, for a $1M revenue restaurant, $10,000 in recovered gross margin. That is a concrete, honest ROI anchor. We do not need to promise magic. Saving 1.5% of food cost — from waste reduction, better ordering, and supplier price visibility — pays for a $199/mo subscription sixty times over.

1.5 Why Canada first (and not the United States)





Smaller, more contained market. ~45,000 addressable independent restaurants and cafés. Big enough to build a real business ($50M+ ARR possible), small enough that a single focused team can dominate the mindshare.





POS concentration is low. Toast has ~1.8% of Canadian POS market vs ~23% in the US. Square leads but Lightspeed (a Canadian company) and TouchBistro (Canadian) have meaningful share. No single POS owns the inventory layer. The integration wedge is wider than in the US.





Bilingual regulatory complexity is a barrier to entry. PIPEDA, Québec Law 25, CRA bookkeeping retention, provincial FoodSafe, HACCP — US-built competitors treat these as an afterthought. We treat them as first-class.





Supplier landscape is duopolistic. Sysco Canada and GFS Canada dominate broadline distribution. Two dominant players is easier to integrate with than the fragmented US distributor market. If we get Sysco and GFS onboarded to our supplier marketplace, we cover 60%+ of our customers&apos; order volume.





Lower CAC. Restaurant associations are provincial and approachable (Restaurants Canada; Ontario Restaurant Hotel &amp; Motel Association; Association Restauration Québec). Association sponsorships deliver qualified leads far cheaper than Google Ads in the US.





It&apos;s where the team is. Local operator interviews, in-kitchen testing, and supplier calls happen in days, not weeks.

3.3 The recipe-to-depletion mechanism



When a sale is inserted, we do not decrement one SKU by one; we walk the recipe tree.



Example. A guest orders 2× &quot;Chicken Shawarma Plate&quot;. The POS webhook fires. The pos-webhook Edge Function inserts a sales row with quantity=2 and menu_item_id matching the POS external ID. An AFTER INSERT trigger on sales calls a stored procedure deplete_from_sale(sale_id) which:





Looks up the active recipe for that menu_item_id (the one whose active_from ≤ sold_at AND active_to IS NULL OR &gt; sold_at).





For each recipe_ingredient, multiplies quantity × sales.quantity. So 180g chicken × 2 plates = 360g chicken total.





If the ingredient is a sub-recipe (e.g., garlic sauce), it recurses: 20g of garlic sauce × 2 plates = 40g sauce, which is then decomposed into yogurt + garlic + lemon using the garlic sauce&apos;s own recipe. Sub-recipes deduct from the sub-recipe&apos;s own stock first (we produced a batch yesterday); only if that stock is exhausted do we deplete the raw ingredients.





Writes a stock_movements row for each deducted ingredient with movement_type=&apos;sale_depletion&apos; and reference_id=sale.id.





Updates inventory_items.current_stock in a single UPDATE per item.





Applies any modifier&apos;s extra_ingredients (e.g., &quot;extra chicken&quot; adds 100g more).



Modifiers matter more than you think



A restaurant that sells 300 &quot;build your own burrito&quot; orders a day cannot model each one as a single menu item. Modifiers (add guac, sub black beans, extra cheese) must compose onto the base recipe at depletion time. Our schema supports this via menu_item_modifiers.extra_ingredients, and the deplete_from_sale procedure applies them in the same pass.



POS integrations must normalize modifiers. Square calls them modifier_sets; Toast calls them modifier_groups; Lightspeed uses options. The webhook handler translates them all into our shape.





3.4 Unit conversion and yield



The hardest part of restaurant inventory is not the BOM — it&apos;s the units.





Three units per item. Every inventory_item has a purchase_unit (how the supplier sells it, e.g., &quot;case of 10kg&quot;), a storage_unit (how the walk-in tracks it, e.g., &quot;kg&quot;), and a recipe_unit (how the recipe calls for it, e.g., &quot;gram&quot;). Factors convert one to another.





Yield adjustments. A 5kg whole chicken does not yield 5kg of usable meat. We let the operator set a yield_percentage (default 100%) on items that are butchered or trimmed. The effective cost per gram of usable meat is unit_cost / (net_weight × yield_percentage). This makes menu item costing accurate.





Prep yields. A batch of tomato sauce that called for 2kg of raw tomato may yield 1.6kg of finished sauce. prep_batches captures produced_qty (1.6kg) and the raw ingredients consumed (2kg tomato, 30g garlic, etc.). Variance between planned and actual produced feeds the variance report.





System-seeded conversions. 1kg = 1000g, 1L = 1000ml, 1lb = 453.592g, 1oz (volume) = 29.5735ml. These live in units_of_measure and are not user-editable. Item-specific conversions (1 case of tomatoes = 25lb) live in unit_conversions and are editable by tenants.








Part 4 — The Forecast Engine



The one thing that matters



If the predictions are accurate, users trust the system and keep paying. If the predictions are wrong, nothing else matters — not the push notifications, not the share sheet, not the benchmarks. Build the forecast engine first. Test it against known data. Everything else is delivery mechanism for the engine&apos;s output.





4.1 What the v2 code got wrong (so we don&apos;t repeat it)



The current agent-daily-run Edge Function claims to run EWMA but doesn&apos;t. It computes an EWMA value in a loop and then throws it away, substituting a flat 90-day mean. The input array is per-sale-event, not per-day. No safety stock is ever calculated. Risk score ignores lead time. Confidence is a field-completeness score, not a statistical one. Fix these in the rewrite.





4.2 The correct forecast pipeline



Five stages, executed nightly at 2 AM per tenant timezone.





Stage 1 — Observe





Pull the last 90 days of sales events for this tenant.





For each inventory item, reconstruct daily consumption from stock_movements (movement_type IN (&apos;sale_depletion&apos;, &apos;waste&apos;, &apos;prep_consumption&apos;)).





Bucket by calendar date in the tenant&apos;s timezone — not UTC.





Store as a dense array: one value per day for each of the last 90 days, with 0 for days of no movement.





Stage 2 — Decompose





Extract day-of-week component: mean consumption by DoW (Mon through Sun).





Extract trend component: linear regression slope over the 90-day window.





Residual = actual − (DoW baseline + trend).





Flag days where |residual| &gt; 3σ as anomalies (holidays, events, stockouts).





Exclude flagged anomaly days from the demand signal used downstream.





Stage 3 — Forecast





Run EWMA (α = 0.3) on the cleaned daily series to get a smooth daily-demand level.





Apply the DoW multipliers on top: next-Friday demand = level × DoW_multiplier[Fri].





Standard deviation σ computed on the residual series (for safety stock).





Safety stock = z × σ × √lead_time, where z = 1.65 for 95% service level (user-adjustable per item).





Reorder point = avg_daily_demand × lead_time + safety_stock.





Days-of-cover = current_stock / avg_daily_demand.





Stage 4 — Score



Metric



Formula



What it tells the user



Risk score (0–100)



Piecewise function of days_of_cover vs lead_days + safety_days. 100 = will run out before supplier can deliver.



How urgently you need to act.

Function of: (a) days of observed data, (b) coefficient of variation of the residuals, (c) number of anomalies excluded. Capped at 95% until item has 60+ days of data.

4.4 When we add the LLM layer



The forecast engine above is pure math. It runs in &lt;100ms per tenant and costs us zero marginal dollars. The LLM layer is an optional enhancement, called only in three situations:





Anomaly explanation. When an anomaly is detected, we call Claude with: &quot;Restaurant X sold 3× the usual chicken on Thursday. Weather: rainy. Local events: [from Google Places]. Explain plausible causes in one sentence.&quot; The output is surfaced in the daily brief.





Prep list generation. Every afternoon at 4 PM, we call Claude with tomorrow&apos;s forecasted menu-item demand plus today&apos;s prep stock levels. Claude outputs a natural-language prep list: &quot;Tomorrow is forecast busy (Friday, clear weather, typical 340 covers). You&apos;ll need 12kg of chicken thawed, 3 batches of tomato sauce, 2L of garlic mayo. Current prep stock: 4kg chicken thawed, no sauce prepped, 0.8L mayo — plan 90 minutes of morning prep.&quot;





Bot command fallback. When the in-app bot cannot parse a command via regex (&quot;hey can you show me what we&apos;re low on except the seasonal stuff&quot;), Claude parses the natural-language query into a structured command and the Edge Function executes it.



Per-tenant LLM cost projection: ~$0.15 CAD/month at average usage (1 anomaly explanation + 30 prep lists + ~10 bot fallbacks), using Claude Haiku for high-frequency calls and Claude Sonnet for prep lists. Even at our $129/mo Pro tier, LLM cost is &lt; 0.2% of revenue.

5.1 The reality of Canadian restaurant procurement



Independent Canadian restaurants typically order from three tiers of suppliers:





Broadline distributors. Sysco Canada and Gordon Food Service (GFS) dominate. 60–75% of a typical menu by volume. Weekly or bi-weekly deliveries. Both have online portals (Sysco Shop, GFS Order Ahead) and account reps. Both publish GS1-encoded product catalogues.





Specialty distributors and local farms. Produce (Ontario Food Terminal vendors, BC farms, Fresh Direct Produce Inc.), seafood (Seacore, Hooked), halal/kosher proteins, organic dairy. Often phone- or text-ordered.





Cash-and-carry. Costco Business, Restaurant Depot, GFS Stores. Owner drives there, buys what&apos;s urgent, expenses it. No PO.



Our integration strategy



v1: iOS Share Sheet sends text/PDF POs to any supplier — works for every tier, zero supplier onboarding needed.



v1.5: Official Sysco and GFS integrations via their ordering APIs (both publish partner programs). Orders placed through us land directly in the distributor&apos;s system. This is the start of the supplier marketplace moat (see Part 9).



v2: Specialty distributor onboarding via a lightweight supplier portal — suppliers see incoming orders from our customers in a dashboard.





5.2 The seven-step flow



Adapted from v2, with Canadian-specific improvements at each step.





Step 1 — Detect (2 AM nightly)





Agent runs forecast. Items with risk &gt; 90 AND confidence &gt; 40 become PO candidates.





Canadian-specific: account for STAT holidays (Canada Day, Labour Day, Thanksgiving, etc.) — suppliers do not deliver on stat days, so effective lead time extends.





Account for supplier delivery days (e.g., GFS delivers to my zone on Tuesdays and Fridays only).





Step 2 — Draft





Group candidates by supplier into one PO per supplier.





Compute order quantity per item: round up to case pack (8-pack, 12-pack, etc. from supplier catalogue).





Apply supplier minimum order amount — if the total is under the minimum, add slower-moving items up to the threshold rather than paying a small-order fee.





Use the last known unit cost from the most recent reconciled invoice; surface if cost has drifted &gt;5% in 30 days.





Generate a PO number: [Business-prefix]-YYYY-NNNN.





Status = DRAFT.





Step 3 — Notify





APNs push to all admin users: &quot;PO-2026-0042 drafted for Sysco — 8 items, $1,240 CAD total. Review?&quot;





In-app bot receives a message card with &quot;Review PO&quot; button.





Badge count on app icon increments.





Step 4 — Review &amp; Approve





User taps the notification or opens the Orders tab.





Sees the draft PO grouped by supplier, items, quantities, costs, total.





Can approve as-is (PIN entry required), edit quantities, remove items, add items, or reject.





The agent learns from every edit — see Step 7.





PIN is 4-digit, bcrypt-hashed at rest, with 3-attempts-then-15-min-lockout. Biometric unlock is Phase 2.

Step 7 — Learn





Lead time adjustment. Sent March 1, delivered March 4 → effective lead time 3 days. If stored lead time was 5, new value = 0.7 × stored + 0.3 × observed = 4.4 days. Slow EMA, no overreacting to one-off delays.





Quantity edit learning. User edited agent&apos;s suggestion from 50 to 30. The delta (−40%) is stored. After 3 consistent negative edits, the agent&apos;s formula for that item gets a correction factor.





Price drift tracking. Every delivered PO updates unit_cost on each item. Sudden jumps &gt;10% surface as a supplier alert: &quot;Chicken thighs up 14% from GFS. Quick compare with Sysco?&quot;





Rejection learning. If the user rejects 2 consecutive POs for an item, raise the auto-draft threshold for that item to risk &gt; 95.

Part 8 — Compliance, Privacy, and Tax (Canadian-Specific)



Canadian SMB software must handle three regulatory vectors from day one. These are not v2 &quot;later&quot; items — they are baked into the schema and UX.





8.1 PIPEDA and Québec Law 25





Data residency. Supabase region set to ca-central-1 (Montréal). All customer data — PII, sales, recipes — stays in Canada. Document this in the privacy policy.





Right of access. Any user can request an export of their data. The data_exports table supports this — admin taps &quot;Export my data&quot;, we generate a ZIP of JSON + CSV within 24 hours (Law 25 requires response within 30 days; ours is next-day).





Right of erasure. Account deletion cascades to all PII while preserving pseudonymized aggregate data for the benchmark network. We disclose this trade-off in plain language during signup (&quot;When you delete, your identifiable data is destroyed within 30 days. Anonymized patterns that can&apos;t identify you remain in our benchmark data.&quot;).





French-language mandate. Under Québec Law 25 and Bill 96, a Québec business must be able to use the service in French. v1.2 ships Québec French. Any Québec tenants in v1.0–v1.1 get a good-faith English service with a French migration notice.





Breach notification. PIPEDA s. 10.1 requires notification of breaches causing &quot;real risk of significant harm&quot; to affected individuals and the Privacy Commissioner. Our incident-response runbook must include 72-hour notification workflow.





8.2 CRA record retention





Six years, plus current. CRA requires businesses to keep all records supporting a tax return for six years from the end of the fiscal year the records relate to. For a restaurant this means sales, invoices, POs, adjustments — everything we store.





Our policy. Default retention of operational data is 7 years (one-year safety buffer). audit_log, sales, invoices, and purchase_orders are never hard-deleted within this window, even on account cancellation. Instead we mark them archived and exclude from UI. Hard delete only after 7+ years.





Export on cancellation. When a tenant cancels, auto-generate a full data export and email it to the admin. Retain the archive in cold storage (Supabase storage, tenant-specific key) until the 7-year mark.





8.3 HST/GST/PST handling





Subscription billing. Stripe Tax computes sales tax on our subscription charges. Canadian provinces have different rates (5% GST in AB, 13% HST in ON, etc.). Stripe handles this — we don&apos;t roll our own.





Restaurant&apos;s own tax. On purchase orders and invoices, we track tax per line (so the operator&apos;s bookkeeping reflects real supplier invoices). POs show subtotal, tax, and total separately. We do not attempt to advise on input tax credits — that&apos;s the accountant&apos;s job.





QuickBooks / Xero export. One-click export of PO and invoice data in QuickBooks-friendly CSV format. Phase 2: native QuickBooks Online API integration (popular in Canada for SMBs).





8.4 Food-safety and labour (Phase 2 hooks)





HACCP awareness. waste_events with reason=&apos;spoiled&apos; or &apos;expired&apos; generate a weekly summary to help operators prepare for health inspections. We do not market as a HACCP system — that&apos;s a regulated product class — but we give operators the data they need.





FoodSafe records. Phase 2: temperature log attachment per location, with threshold alerts. We defer this to v1.5 to stay focused on inventory in v1.





Labour costing. Phase 2: pull labour hours from POS or scheduling apps (7shifts, Push Operations) to produce prime-cost reports (food + labour as % of revenue). Essential for restaurant P&amp;L but out of v1 scope.








Part 9 — The Moat Playbook



Why this section is the most important one in the document



If we ship everything in Parts 1–8 and call it a day, we have a nice product. Toast, Square, or a well-funded entrant can clone it in a quarter. A moat means that every day in market, the product becomes harder to replicate — not by adding features, but by accumulating advantages that a new entrant cannot easily buy their way around. This document commits to three of them, layered.





9.1 Moat Layer 1 — Data Network Effects (Benchmark Network)





The thesis



Every independent restaurant operator is flying blind on one question: &quot;is my number good?&quot; They look at their 31% food cost and don&apos;t know whether that&apos;s great or terrible for their format. Toast and Square won&apos;t tell them because they don&apos;t have the cross-restaurant data in a format that can be benchmarked. MarketMan has some, but doesn&apos;t surface it meaningfully. We turn this into our first moat by collecting, normalizing, and peer-anonymizing every operator&apos;s data from day one and showing them where they stand — with enough granularity to act on.

How we protect it (so competitors can&apos;t just scrape it)





k-anonymity. No benchmark is shown unless at least 10 peers are in the cohort. Less than 10 → we show a wider cohort or a diplomatic &quot;not enough peers yet, check back&quot;.





Differential privacy on rare SKUs. For long-tail SKUs, add a small random noise term before surfacing price percentiles, so no single peer&apos;s data can be backed out.





Consent is explicit. At onboarding we show: &quot;Join the benchmark network: contribute your (anonymized) data to get access to peer comparisons. You can opt out anytime — if you do, you also stop receiving benchmark features.&quot; Most operators will opt in for the benefit. A small percentage won&apos;t, and that&apos;s fine.





Tenant-leaving-doesn&apos;t-break-it. Cancelled accounts&apos; historical data stays in the benchmark (de-identified). So the network grows monotonically even with churn.





Why this is a moat



A competitor launching next quarter starts with zero peer data. Their benchmarks are empty for 6–12 months while they bootstrap. For an operator, a product with a living benchmark network is strictly better than one with an empty cohort grid. The gap compounds: our 100th customer helps the 101st, whose data helps the 102nd. After 300 Canadian café customers, our GTA café cohort is the single best source of comparative data in the country, and rebuilding it is a two-year grind for any entrant.

9.3 Moat Layer 3 — Proprietary Forecasting





The thesis



A vanilla EWMA model is not a moat — any engineer can build it in a day. What becomes defensible is a forecasting stack that (a) outperforms generic models on restaurant data specifically, (b) gets better as we accumulate more data, and (c) incorporates signals no competitor has access to.





The three models, in order of ambition





Model 1 — Hierarchical Bayesian forecast (ship in v1.5)





A per-SKU, per-tenant forecast that shrinks toward a tenant-type prior (e.g., &quot;cafés like you&quot;).





Handles cold-start gracefully because the prior has mass even when the tenant has 2 days of data.





Updates Bayesian posteriors nightly as new data arrives.





Outperforms raw EWMA by 15–30% on MAPE for items with &lt;60 days of history (our measurement target).





Implementable in a Supabase Edge Function using lightweight sampling, or offloaded to a small Python worker on Fly.io / Railway.





Model 2 — External signal ingestion (ship in v2.0)





Weather. Environment Canada forecast API (free, no key needed). Rain in Toronto drops café foot traffic 12–18% day-of. We use local forecasts to adjust tomorrow&apos;s demand.





Local events. Ticketmaster / Eventbrite / SeatGeek scraping for venue events within 500m. A Raptors game shifts a nearby sports bar&apos;s demand pattern.





Statistics Canada CPI &amp; Food Price Index. When beef CPI jumps 8%, substitution effects cascade — we surface these early.





Holidays &amp; Canadian cultural calendar. Mother&apos;s Day demand spike, Nuit Blanche, Caribana, Celebration of Light, Calgary Stampede. Each has a predictable shape by venue type.





Google Places foot-traffic signal. Hourly busyness index for the operator&apos;s own location and nearby competitors. We blend this with sales data for a leading indicator.





Model 3 — Cross-tenant transfer learning (ship in v3.0, year 2+)





A neural network trained on our entire fleet&apos;s (anonymized) sales time series.





For any new tenant, the model initializes from a learned &quot;restaurant demand manifold&quot; — it already knows the shape of Friday-night-Italian-QSR-demand because it&apos;s seen 80 of them.





This is the feature that becomes existentially hard for competitors to replicate. They need our scale of anonymized time-series data. Without it, their cold-start is markedly worse than ours forever.





How we communicate this to the operator (without overselling)





Transparency by default. Every forecast shows a confidence band and a short explanation: &quot;Forecast for Fri: 42 servings of shawarma (90% range: 34–52). Reason: typical Friday pattern + rain forecast drops dine-in 15%.&quot; This beats the black-box feel of competitors.





We publish our MAPE. Quarterly, we post our aggregate forecast accuracy (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) publicly. This is a marketing asset (nobody else does this) and an honesty device (it keeps us accountable to ourselves).





Why the three moats compound



Benchmarks require data, and the forecast model produces calibrated per-SKU demand history which feeds benchmarks. The supplier marketplace generates price-time-series data that feeds both the benchmarks and the forecast model (input cost elasticity). The forecast model&apos;s superior accuracy is the headline feature that wins new restaurant customers, which grows the benchmark network, which justifies more supplier integrations. A competitor would have to solve all three simultaneously from zero.

Part 14 — Decision Summary





14.1 What changed from v2



Removed: all WhatsApp code paths, Arabic/RTL, Egyptian POS integrations (Foodics/Geidea/Fawry), EGP as default currency, pharmacy sample data, and the generic multi-vertical positioning.



Added: recipe &amp; BOM subsystem, unit conversions, waste tracking, Canadian supplier integrations (Sysco, GFS), HST/GST handling, PIPEDA data residency in ca-central-1, 20-minute guided onboarding, Bayesian forecasting, three-layer moat (benchmarks + supplier marketplace + proprietary forecasting), and Canadian compliance (Québec Law 25, CRA 7-year retention).



Kept: RLS-first Supabase backend, PIN approval flow, audit log, cold-start blending, iOS Share Sheet, native push, in-app bot, no-LLM-for-hot-path principle.





14.2 The core bet



Independent Canadian restaurant operators will pay $129/month for an app that (a) drafts their orders to Sysco and GFS automatically, (b) tells them honestly how they compare to peer restaurants, and (c) forecasts accurately enough that they stop running out of chicken on Friday nights. None of these are individually a moat. Together, over 24 months, they compound into one: we own the benchmark network, we own the supplier integrations, and we own the forecasting model that every competitor has to build from scratch.





14.3 The one thing that matters



The forecast engine is still the heart of everything. If the predictions are accurate, users trust the system and keep paying. If the predictions are wrong, nothing else matters — not the benchmarks, not the marketplace, not the Sysco integration, not the variance report. Build the forecast engine correctly. Test it against real data. Everything else is delivery mechanism for what that engine produces.
